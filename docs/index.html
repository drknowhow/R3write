New in 1.5

Autocorrect, system-wide and offline

The rewrite hotkey handles the paragraph you meant to write. Autocorrect handles the word you didn't. It watches typing in the applications you list, and when you finish a word with a space, punctuation or Enter, it checks that word against a dictionary bundled inside the app — 82,833 entries, no network, no model, no key — and fixes it in place.

  • Off until you turn it on. The installer offers it on an Optional features page, unchecked. That records a preference and nothing more — the keyboard hook is never installed until the app has both an active license and an explicit switch in Settings → Autocorrect.
  • Your applications, not ours. Add apps by picking them from a list of what's running, by name, rather than knowing that Outlook is outlook.exe. The shipped list is Notepad, WordPad, Word and Outlook. Nothing outside your list is ever read.
  • Undo that expires on purpose. A toast shows the correction with an Undo button; Ctrl+Alt+Z reverts the last one whether the toast is still up or not. Both retire the moment reverting would land in the wrong place.
  • Ranked by how typos actually happen. Candidates are scored by corpus frequency weighted by the shape of the edit — transpositions and doubled keystrokes first, adjacent-key slips next. Genuinely ambiguous words are left alone, and words that look like intended plurals are never quietly made singular.
  • Keystrokes are never written to disk. The typing buffer lives in memory, is capped, and is cleared on every invalidation. Corrections — not keystrokes — are logged, and the log is yours to clear.

Where it refuses to run

Autocorrect fails closed. When a check can't answer, that counts as a refusal — silence is never read as permission.

Password fields

Two independent checks — the Win32 field style and a UI Automation probe. Either one saying "password" refuses, and neither being able to answer refuses too.

Elevated windows

A non-elevated app cannot send input to an elevated one, so those are skipped outright.

Remote desktop

The shadow buffer tracks local keystrokes; a remote session's text isn't local.

IME and CJK input

Composition-based input has no single committed word to correct.

Browsers and Electron apps

For now. Windows only reports "this is a password field" for native text boxes, and Chromium draws its own — so the refusal is conservative until that gap closes.

Terminals — allowed, with a warning

You can add cmd.exe or powershell.exe; they're flagged and off the default list. A shell password prompt isn't a password field, so nothing can detect it — and Enter runs the line, so a correction landing on a path or a flag executes something you didn't type.

AI transparency

R3write is an AI system, and it is built with AI. Both are worth saying plainly rather than leaving you to infer them.

Its output is machine-generated

Every rewrite — Improve, Fix grammar, Shorten, Expand, Tone, Prompt, Custom — is produced by a large language model, not by rules. Models restate things you didn't say, drop qualifiers that mattered, and are confidently wrong. Read the rewrite before you accept it. That is what the diff view is for.

The app says so where the output appears, not only here: the quick-edit popup carries a permanent AI-generated · review before use line.

What leaves your machine

There are exactly three paths, and you choose which are live:

  1. Local Ollama — nothing leaves. The model runs on your machine.
  2. A cloud provider — the text you selected and your prompt go to the provider you configured, using your key, over a direct connection. R3write operates no server and proxies nothing.
  3. Autocorrect's contextual check — off by default, and separate from the autocorrect switch. When on, a single confusable word plus up to 80 characters of preceding context goes to your configured provider. It can only ever suggest; applying is a button you press.

The offline autocorrect dictionary sends nothing anywhere. There is no telemetry, no analytics, and no usage reporting in any path.

R3write was built with AI

The application itself — its Rust and TypeScript, this website, and much of its documentation — was written by its author working with AI coding assistants. Every change was reviewed and is the author's responsibility, but the code is not hand-typed from scratch and saying otherwise would be misleading.

If you publish what you rewrite

Under the EU AI Act's transparency rules, text generated or materially altered by AI and published to inform the public on a matter of public interest must be labelled as such by whoever publishes it — unless a person has genuinely reviewed it and taken editorial responsibility. R3write cannot do that for you; it happens at the moment you publish.

About R3write

R3write started as a personal itch: every time I wanted to clean up a paragraph, tighten a Slack message, or rewrite a sentence I'd just typed, I'd Alt+Tab into a chatbot, paste, copy the response, switch back, and lose my train of thought. That tab-switching tax adds up — and for the small rewrites I do constantly, a 30-second chat is overkill.

R3write replaces the tab switch with a hotkey. Select text in any app, press Ctrl+Alt+G, pick an action, paste back. The rewrite happens where you're already typing.

Principles

  • Local by default. The free path uses Ollama on your machine. No network, no key, no quota.
  • Bring your own key. Every cloud provider uses your API key directly. R3write doesn't proxy anything through its own server. There is no R3write server.
  • No telemetry. R3write doesn't phone home, doesn't track usage, doesn't collect analytics.
  • Your data stays where you put it. Selections go to the provider you configured. That's it.
